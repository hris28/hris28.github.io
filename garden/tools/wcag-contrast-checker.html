What actually makes text "readable enough"?

10 Feb 2026 · accessibility, design, tool

In my design work, I'd frequently pick two colors that looked fine, then wonder whether they were actually legible for someone with low vision. It turns out there's a precise, boring, wonderful answer: ✨a formula✨. So I built a little checker to stop guessing.

How it actually works

Contrast here isn't about hue, but rather about relative luminance: the perceived brightness of a color normalized so black is 0 and white is 1. For the sRGB colors screens use, luminance is a weighted sum that leans heavily on green (because our eyes do too): L = 0.2126·R + 0.7152·G + 0.0722·B, after each channel is "linearized" from its raw 0-255 value.[2]

The contrast ratio between two colors is then (L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05), where L1 is the lighter color and L2 the darker. Ratios run from 1:1 (identical) up to 21:1 (pure black on pure white).[1]

The thresholds

First, the vocabulary. WCAG stands for the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, the international standard published by the W3C for making web content usable by people with disabilities. It defines three conformance levels, which are simply tiers of strictness:[3]

  • Level A is the bare minimum: skip it and some people genuinely cannot use the content. For contrast specifically, level A sets no ratio requirement at all.
  • Level AA is the practical target almost everyone aims for. It is what the ADA, US Section 508, the European EN 301 549 standard, and most company policies require. If you remember one number, aim for AA.
  • Level AAA is the enhanced, strictest tier. It is excellent when you can reach it, but the W3C itself does not recommend requiring AAA across a whole site, because some content cannot meet it. Treat it as a stretch goal, not a baseline.

The other distinction is normal versus large text. Bigger letters carry more visual signal, so they stay readable at lower contrast and get an easier bar. WCAG counts text as large when it is at least 18pt (about 24px) regular, or 14pt (about 18.66px) bold; anything smaller counts as normal.[1]

Putting those together, here is what each badge in the checker is testing:

  • AA normal: 4.5:1 - the everyday default for body text.
  • AA large: 3:1 - headings and other large text passing the standard bar.
  • AAA normal: 7:1 - body text at the enhanced bar.
  • AAA large: 4.5:1 - large text at the enhanced bar.

Large, heavier letters stay legible at lower contrast, which is why every "large" bar is gentler than its "normal" counterpart. The thresholds are hard floors. A computed 4.499:1 does not pass 4.5:1, as you do not round up.[1]

Why these numbers? They're tied to vision loss: 4.5:1 compensates for roughly 20/40 acuity (common around age 80), and 7:1 for about 20/80.[1] The math isn't arbitrary; it represents real people squinting at real screens.
